## 1. 2027 Labor Ministry (MOEL) budget — confirmed

Total MOEL 2027 budget proposal: **₩38.9537 trillion**, up 3.4%
(+₩1.2776T) from the 2026 budget. Three priority areas: "미래" (future —
AI-transition job response), "포용" (inclusion — reducing labor-market
polarization), "안심" (safety/security — safe, happy workplaces).

Within that: **₩3.8B ("38억원") for an industrial-safety AI assistant**
service — confirmed as a specific new line item.

Foreign-worker-specific new budget items in the same proposal:
- ₩0.25B ("2억5000만원") — human-rights-leader program to prevent rights
  violations against foreign workers
- ₩1.2B ("12억원") — basic safety/health education for foreign workers
- ₩1.0B ("10억원") — specialized industrial-accident-prevention support

No single line item in these results is labeled "worker-reporting
technology" or "site demonstrations." The deck's **₩2B** half of the
combined ₩5.8B figure is closest to the ₩1.2B + ₩1.0B pair above (₩2.2B),
which is Peter's own framing, not a single confirmed budget line.

## 2. 286 construction-industry fatalities, 2025 — confirmed

MOEL's own 2025 industrial-accident statistics (published March 2026):
construction fatalities rose to **286** (+10, +3.6% year-on-year). Total
industrial fatalities subject to investigation across **all** industries
were 605 in 2025 (+16, +2.7% from 589 in 2024). 286 is the construction
subset of the 605 total — the two numbers are consistent, not competing
claims (this resolves the ambiguity flagged in the 2026-09-24 research
pass, which had found the 605 figure without the construction-specific
breakdown).

## 2b. The actual 2026 employer-duty amendment — confirmed, with a date

A second research pass (2026-09-25, prompted by a request to focus specifically
on this year's amendment) found the real legislative event: the **산업안전보건법
(Occupational Safety and Health Act) amendment passed the National Assembly
plenary session on June 18, 2026.** It creates a new employer obligation —
previously absent from the law — to ensure foreign workers they hire complete
**기초안전보건교육 (basic safety/health education)**. The stated rationale
matches the deck's framing almost exactly: foreign workers face higher
industrial-accident risk due to language barriers and unfamiliar workplaces,
and had insufficient access to training before this.

The same bill also **mandates electronic card terminals (전자카드 단말기) at
construction sites** for foreign workers — a companion attendance/access-
tracking requirement, not just a training mandate. Violations carry a fine of
up to **₩3,000,000**. The provision takes effect **six months after
promulgation**.

This should replace the deck's looser "2 days before this pitch" framing —
the confirmed, citable event is the June 18, 2026 National Assembly passage,
not a specific September 22 publication (see §3 below, still unconfirmed).
Note also a broader, separate OSH Act revision (partial, February 19, 2026)
adding employer risk-assessment and safety-disclosure duties — general
context, not specific to foreign workers, but part of the same 2026
regulatory wave.

## 6. Cultural and structural context: why reporting is hard in practice

Requested 2026-09-25: how Korean construction companies actually operate,
and why a hazard genuinely goes unreported even where the law nominally
requires safety measures. This context should inform the product's design
assumptions (anonymity, escalation paths), not just its slide copy.

**Multi-layer subcontracting blurs who's responsible.** Korean construction
runs on a 원청 (primary/general contractor) → 하청 (subcontractor) chain,
often several layers deep. Research on this structure describes the core
failure mode plainly: work instructions, safety training, PPE provision,
and hazard-information sharing all get diffused across layers, and on
mixed-work sites, if which company is doing which dangerous work in which
space isn't shared in real time, accident risk rises sharply. The highest-
risk work — working at height, confined-space welding, heavy-material
handling, painting — concentrates on subcontracted (하청) workers, while
the authority and budget to actually fix a hazard sits with the 원청.
Even where a 원청 has a safety-management system on paper, if it doesn't
functionally reach the worker at the bottom of the chain, it doesn't
operate as a real safety net. Foreign workers, disproportionately employed
by subcontractors, sit at the bottom of exactly this structure.

**The 원청/하청 relationship is legally recognized as hierarchical.**
Subcontracting regulation in Korea is explicitly framed around preventing
unfair practices arising from "일방적 지배와 복종관계" (a one-sided
dominance-and-subordination relationship) between primary and
subcontracting parties — i.e., the vertical power imbalance isn't just a
cultural observation, it's the acknowledged premise behind why
subcontracting-fairness law exists at all.

**E-9 (Employment Permit System) visa status creates a strong disincentive
to report.** A migrant worker on an E-9 visa can generally change employers
only a limited number of times (three within an initial contract, two more
under an extension), and a transfer requires either the current employer's
consent or proof the employer violated the law — proof that's notoriously
hard to establish given language barriers and lack of documentation. This
gives the employer significant structural leverage: a worker who leaves a
workplace without permission is classified a "runaway" and becomes
undocumented. In practice, this is a real chilling effect on any reporting
that might be seen as making trouble for the employer — a genuinely
different risk calculus than a Korean national worker filing the same
report. (Separately, a 2024 Ministry of Justice change means officials are
no longer required to report undocumented workers who come forward to
claim unpaid wages — a precedent for "safe channel to report without
immigration consequence," though it's specific to wage claims, not
established for safety reports.)

**Implication for the product**: this is direct support for keeping hazard
reports free of any language/identity marker visible to a worker's
immediate supervisor (already implemented, 2026-09-25) — in a context
where visa status itself depends on employer goodwill, minimizing what
identifies a specific worker to the people with power over their
employment isn't just good privacy practice, it's what makes the tool
usable at all for the population it's built for.
