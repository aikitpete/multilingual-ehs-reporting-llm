What the jury will probably say

A hackathon-jury simulation for AnjeonTok — the kind of panel judging a HABSIDA-style event: a mix of government/labor-ministry evaluators, a construction-industry rep, and a startup/tech mentor. Written to be genuinely critical, not reassuring — the questions here are the ones most likely to actually get asked, with a suggested answer for each.

1 What they'll likely praise
The legal research is unusually rigorousCiting the exact law (OSH Act amendment, June 18, 2026), the exact penalty (₩3M fine), and being upfront about what's confirmed vs. your own framing is not typical hackathon-team diligence — judges notice this.
The worker-language-hiding decisionHiding a worker's language from their supervisor, justified by real E-9 visa-dependency research, reads as genuine ethical design rather than a bolted-on privacy feature.
"One report, four outputs" is a strong core insightIt's the single clearest, most memorable idea in the whole pitch — a judge will likely repeat it back to you.
The governance modelMapping six actors' legal obligations before writing a feature list is the kind of thinking judges associate with teams who'll actually survive a real deployment, not just a demo day.
2 The one question you most need a sharp answer for

"KOSHA already gives away multilingual safety materials for free, in up to 20 languages. Why does a construction company pay for this instead of just using that?"

This is the single most dangerous question because it's true — your own Knowledge page cites it. A weak answer here undermines the whole pitch.

Suggested answer: KOSHA's materials are static — posters, PDFs, pre-recorded videos. They tell a worker what a hazard looks like in general. AnjeonTok is the missing other direction: a worker reporting a hazard they just found, in their own words, right now, in their own language — and that report becoming a Korean report, a printed label, and a compliance record automatically. KOSHA solves awareness; AnjeonTok solves the reporting gap that awareness alone doesn't close. They're complementary, not competing — AnjeonTok's Learn tab explicitly repackages real incidents into the same kind of training content KOSHA distributes.

3 Toughest questions, by category
Feasibility

"This is a hackathon. Where's the actual AI? Every translation, classification, and report here is hand-written mock data."

Answer: Be honest, don't oversell. "Correct — this is a UX and workflow prototype, not a working ML pipeline. The hard technical problem (accurate speech-to-text and translation for low-resource languages like Uzbek, in noisy construction-site audio) is real and we haven't solved it yet. What we've validated is the workflow and the governance model — who sees what, who's liable for what, what the output needs to look like for a manager and for government. That's the part that doesn't change once the AI is real."
Business model

"Who actually pays — the general contractor (원청) or the subcontractor (하청)? You haven't decided."

Answer: "The 원청, because they hold the site-wide budget and authority to fix hazards, and they carry the SAPA liability exposure regardless of who employs the worker who got hurt. Subcontractors wouldn't be the buyer — they're often the ones with the least slack to pay for anything. This also means the sales motion is one deal per site, not per employer, which is simpler to scale."
Ethics / social impact

"A tool the employer buys and controls — does it actually protect a worker who's afraid of losing their visa, or does it just give the employer another compliance checkbox while the worker still doesn't trust it?"

Answer: This one has no fully satisfying answer yet — acknowledge that. "That tension is real and we don't think a UI decision alone solves it. What we've done is remove the specific piece of information (language) that would let a supervisor identify who filed a report. What we haven't done is validate with an actual migrant-worker advocacy group whether that's enough to earn trust, or whether they'd want something structurally different, like reports routed through a third party instead of the employer. That's the first thing we'd do before a real pilot."
Legal / liability

"If your AI misclassifies a hazard as low severity and someone gets hurt, who's liable — you, or the company?"

Answer: "The system never auto-closes or auto-resolves a report without a human decision — severity is worker-proposed and supervisor-confirmed, not machine-decided. AnjeonTok is explicitly not a system of record for legal compliance evidence on its own; it's an input to the human safety-management process the law already requires. That's a deliberate governance boundary, not an oversight."
Numbers

"You cite ₩5.8B in budget support, but your own Knowledge page admits only ₩3.8B is a real single line item. Isn't that a bit of a stretch?"

Answer: Don't get defensive — agree with the premise. "Correct, and we'd rather you catch that from us than from a fact-check later. ₩3.8B is a confirmed line item for exactly this kind of AI safety assistant. The other ₩2B is our own bundling of two adjacent foreign-worker safety budget items — real money, real intent, just not one official number. We show that distinction on our own Knowledge page rather than smoothing it over."
Differentiation

"How is this different from an enterprise EHS (environment/health/safety) platform a big contractor might already use?"

Answer: "Enterprise EHS platforms are built for the safety manager, in Korean, assuming the reporter already speaks the language of the system. AnjeonTok's entire premise is the opposite: the reporter is the one person in the chain who can't use a Korean-only tool. We're not competing to be the safety manager's dashboard — we're the missing intake layer that feeds one."
4 Before you're on stage
  • Rehearse the KOSHA question out loud. It's the one that can derail the pitch if you're caught flat-footed — see §2.
  • Decide, and say out loud, who the buyer is (원청) before someone asks — don't work it out live on stage.
  • Lead with the CEO-liability line, but land it as fact, not scare tactic. State it once, plainly, and move on — dwelling on it reads as manipulative.
  • Have one honest sentence ready for "is this real AI or a mockup." Judges respect an honest "not yet, here's what we validated instead" far more than a dodge.
  • Don't claim the migrant-worker trust question is solved. Naming it as the next validation step is more credible than pretending the UI decision alone fixes it.
